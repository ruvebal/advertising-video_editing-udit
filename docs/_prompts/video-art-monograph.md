# Video Art: Screen, Space, Signal

<figure class="quote">
<blockquote>
Video art is not only something you watch. It is something that organizes time, space, bodies, and attention.
</blockquote>
<figcaption>
&mdash; Seminar synthesis
</figcaption>
</figure>

This monograph gives students a short working definition of **video art**, a compact genealogy, a basic analysis toolkit, and a set of **live examples** that can be screened or assigned quickly.

---

## 1. What Makes Video Art Distinct?

Video art is not simply cinema moved into a museum. It is a **time-based art form** that treats moving images as material: **signal, feedback, loop, monitor, projection, installation, and viewer position**.

Three useful distinctions:

- **Film** often privileges narrative and fixed spectatorship.
- **Television** privileges broadcast flow and mass address.
- **Video art** often privileges experiment, spatial display, repetition, embodiment, and critical use of the screen itself.

In teaching terms, students should ask not only **What does it show?** but also:

- Where is the screen?
- How long does the loop run?
- Is the viewer seated, passing, surrounded, or reflected?
- Does the work behave like a document, a performance, an installation, an essay, or a sabotage of media language?

---

## 2. Fast Genealogy

Use this compact sequence in class:

1. **Television as object**: artists begin by treating TV as image, machine, and ideology.
2. **Video as language**: editing, feedback, playback, and signal become expressive tools.
3. **Performance and identity**: the camera becomes a mirror, witness, and political surface.
4. **Installation and environment**: video leaves the single monitor and occupies architectural space.
5. **Networked image culture**: video art expands into archives, public screens, online circulation, and post-internet conditions.

This makes video art a strong bridge topic between **experimental cinema**, **performance**, **feminist media critique**, **installation**, and **digital culture**.

---

## 3. How to Read a Video Artwork

Keep analysis simple and repeatable. Ask students to track five things:

| Lens               | Key question                                                                   |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Screen**         | Is the screen a window, a mirror, a monitor, or an object?                     |
| **Body**           | What does the work do with gesture, identity, performance, or the gaze?        |
| **Space**          | Is this meant for a single channel, a room, a loop, or an installation?        |
| **Time**           | Does it build through duration, repetition, interruption, slowness, or rhythm? |
| **Media critique** | Does it reuse, parody, appropriate, or expose mass-media language?             |

Short rule for students: **description first, interpretation second**.
Example: "The clip repeats the transformation three times" before "The work criticizes television femininity."

## 4. Live Examples for Class

Start with these. They are the most useful public-facing examples from the attached monograph because they are reachable online and easy to discuss in class.

### 1. Nam June Paik - _Paik's Video Study_ (ongoing archive)

- **Why use it:** Best entry point for the idea of video as archive, experiment, and artist-built media language.
- **Use in class:** Ask students to build a 3-work mini-playlist and explain the order.
- **Live link:** [Paik's Video Study](https://njpart.ggcf.kr/pages/njpvideo)

### 2. Martha Rosler - _Semiotics of the Kitchen_ (1975, excerpt)

- **Why use it:** Clean, direct example of parody, feminist critique, gesture, and television format sabotage.
- **Use in class:** Perfect for a first close reading of body, props, framing, and repetition.
- **Live link:** [MoMA excerpt](https://www.moma.org/multimedia/embed/video/122/800)

### 3. Joan Jonas - _Layers of Time_ (2016) + _Vertical Roll_ (1972)

- **Why use it:** Excellent bridge between performance, feedback, glitch, and feminist video history.
- **Use in class:** Show the interview first, then compare it with a clip/documentation of _Vertical Roll_.
- **Live links:** [Joan Jonas: Layers of Time](https://vimeo.com/161898589) and [Vertical Roll clip](https://www.dailymotion.com/video/x9crft6)

### 4. Pipilotti Rist - _Color is Dangerous_ (2016 interview)

- **Why use it:** Helps students understand video art as affect, color, body, intimacy, and installation thinking.
- **Use in class:** Good when students assume video art must be austere or purely theoretical.
- **Live link:** [Pipilotti Rist interview](https://vimeo.com/167880482)

### 5. Shirin Neshat - _Advice to the Young_ (2018 interview)

- **Why use it:** Strong introduction to political voice, image ethics, and the relation between art and public meaning.
- **Use in class:** Pair with discussion of how video art moves between gallery space and geopolitical discourse.
- **Live link:** [Louisiana Channel interview](https://channel.louisiana.dk/video/shirin-neshat-advice-to-the-young)

### 6. Cecilia Condit - _Possibly in Michigan_ (1983)

- **Why use it:** Shows how video art can be narrative, uncanny, musical, and disturbing at the same time.
- **Use in class:** Good for discussing cult circulation, tone, performance, and internet afterlives.
- **Content note:** unsettling tone, violence.
- **Live link:** [Vimeo](https://vimeo.com/44030303)

### 7. Bruce Nauman - _Good Boy Bad Boy_ (1985)

- **Why use it:** Excellent for teaching language, repetition, emotional escalation, and two-channel structure in video art.
- **Use in class:** Use it to discuss how performance, syntax, and slight asynchrony can generate tension without conventional narrative editing.
- **Live link:** [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=R9BW2SraYeo)
- **Institutional reference:** [Tate](https://www.tate.org.uk/art/artworks/nauman-good-boy-bad-boy-t06853)

## 5. Five Short Video Art Works for University Video Editing Students

This shortlist is designed for editing students: every work is short, technically teachable, and useful for discussing **repetition, performance-to-camera, sound-image tension, appropriation, or medium-specific video effects**.

**Best first pick:** _Technology/Transformation: Wonder Woman_ (1978-79). It is one of the clearest early lessons in how **repetition, looping, and re-ordering** can turn television flow into critique.

### 1. Dara Birnbaum - _Technology/Transformation: Wonder Woman_ (1978-79)

- **Duration:** 5:50
- **Public viewing:** [Dailymotion](https://www.dailymotion.com/video/x4y5e5)
- **Institutional reference:** [MoMA collection record](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/114400)
- **Why it matters:** canonical appropriation work; turns broadcast spectacle into an analysis of gender, power, and televisual repetition.
- **Editing lesson:** loop structure, repeated gesture, sampled sound, tempo through re-ordering.
- **Class exercise:** make a 60-90 second repeat-edit study from one source clip, changing meaning only through repetition, order, and sound.

### 2. Martha Rosler - _Semiotics of the Kitchen_ (1975)

- **Duration:** 6:09
- **Public viewing:** [UbuWeb](https://www.ubu.com/film/rosler_semiotics.html)
- **Institutional reference:** [MoMA excerpt](https://www.moma.org/multimedia/embed/video/122/800)
- **Why it matters:** a foundational feminist video performance that shows how structure and gesture can replace heavy montage.
- **Editing lesson:** internal rhythm, escalation, prop choreography, and fixed framing as a form of temporal composition.
- **Class exercise:** produce a one-shot or minimal-cut micro-work where meaning comes from action sequencing, not shot variety.

### 3. Nancy Holt - _Underscan_ (1974)

- **Duration:** 9:12
- **Public viewing:** [Dailymotion](https://www.dailymotion.com/video/x8kcgc5)
- **Institutional reference:** [Holt/Smithson Foundation](https://holtsmithsonfoundation.org/nancy-holt-underscan-1974)
- **Why it matters:** a strong example of medium-specific video thinking: still photographs become unstable through underscanning, while voice-over compresses years of correspondence.
- **Editing lesson:** sound-led structure, serial stills, time compression, and a repeated image transformation as formal logic.
- **Class exercise:** edit a short "letters-to-video" piece from still images plus voice-over, using one recurring transformation as the work's visual signature.

### 4. Pipilotti Rist - _I'm Not The Girl Who Misses Much_ (1986)

- **Duration:** circulating versions vary; commonly under 10 minutes
- **Public viewing:** [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hjvWXiUp1hI)
- **Institutional reference:** [Louisiana Channel interview context](https://vimeo.com/167880482)
- **Why it matters:** compact, bodily, distorted, and pop-aware; perfect for showing how video art can construct affect through timing and instability rather than plot.
- **Editing lesson:** speed change, repetition, audio distortion, and deliberate sync breakdown.
- **Class exercise:** shoot one repeated gesture and create two versions: one sync-driven, one sync-broken through speed ramps and audio manipulation.

### 5. Richard Serra - _Hand Catching Lead_ (1968)

- **Duration:** about 3 minutes
- **Public viewing:** [UbuWeb](https://www.ubu.com/film/serra_lead.html)
- **Why it matters:** a near-zero edit work that teaches duration, repetition, and micro-variation inside a fixed frame.
- **Editing lesson:** static framing, endurance, rhythm without cutting, and the meaning of minimal event.
- **Class exercise:** shoot a 2-4 minute one-shot repetitive action, then make a second version that changes only the sound track.

**Estimated total runtime:** about 32 minutes.

These are especially useful when you want museum-grade framing, metadata, or analysis around a work:

## 7. Teaching Use

### One-session seminar flow

1. Define video art in one sentence: **screen + time + space + viewer**.
2. Screen two short examples with different logics:
   Rosler for parody/media critique, and Jonas or Rist for embodiment/perception.
3. Ask students to describe before interpreting:
   screen, body, space, time, media critique.
4. End with one comparison question:
   how would this work change if shown on TikTok, in a cinema, or in a museum room?

### Fast assignment prompt

Ask students to choose one live example and write **250-400 words** answering:

- What does the work do with the screen?
- How does time operate: loop, repetition, interruption, buildup?
- Is the work closer to performance, essay, installation, protest, archive, or media sabotage?
- What changes if we imagine it in another display context?

---

## 8. Key Takeaways

1. Video art is not defined only by content but by **display logic** and **viewer position**.
2. It often works through **loop, repetition, feedback, appropriation, and installation**.
3. Many canonical works are also critiques of **television, gender roles, and mass-media language**.
4. The best way to teach it is comparatively: one work about **media critique**, one about **embodiment**, one about **space**.
5. Students need both **live examples** and **stable museum/distributor references**.
